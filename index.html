Module 01 · Lecture 01

Intro to Professional Skills

Overview

This was the first lesson of the module. We learned about the course structure and what "Professional Skills" really mean. It is not just about technical knowledge; it is about how we work with others, understand ourselves, and behave in the workplace. We also looked at the Johari Window and the difference between technical and soft skills.

1. Course Overview & Assessment

This module is 100% continuous assessment. Attendance is compulsory.

30%
Assignment 1
Preparing a CV, Cover Letter, and facing a Mock Interview. (Note: Marks will be 0 for AI-generated documents).
20%
Assignment 2
Group Project (e.g., Food Festival) testing collaborative team execution and operational reporting.
50%
Assignment 3
Self Reflective Portfolio and Viva presentation showcasing semester-long growth and skill synthesis.
2. What are Professional Skills?

Professional skills (also called soft skills or employability skills) are the non-technical abilities we need to succeed at work. They make us a valuable asset to employers because they can be used in any job or industry.

Core Employability Skills:

Communication · Teamwork · Problem-Solving · Adaptability · Time Management · Leadership

Classifying Workplace Skills (Activity 1):
Technical Skills
Specific job knowledge (e.g., Java Programming, Microsoft Excel, Data Analysis).
Soft Skills
Interpersonal capabilities (e.g., Teamwork, Conflict Resolution, Empathy).
Transferable Skills
Versatile skills you can use across any job (e.g., Problem-Solving, Adaptability).
3. Values, Beliefs, Attitudes, and Character

Our professional behavior starts from inside. We learned the "Professional Identity Tree" framework:

Roots · Values
What you think is important (e.g., Honesty, Integrity, Respect). They guide our decisions.
Trunk · Beliefs
What you think is true (e.g., Growth Mindset, Self-Efficacy, Optimism). They shape how we see the world.
Branches · Attitudes
How you express yourself in thought, words, and actions (e.g., Positive Attitude, Open-mindedness, Empathy).
Fruits · Character
The moral qualities others see in you (e.g., Trustworthiness, Responsibility, Fairness).
4. The Johari Window (Luft & Ingham, 1955)

This is a psychological model to help us understand self-awareness. It has four distinct areas:

Open Arena
What you know about yourself and what others know (e.g., your public skills, shared behaviors).
Blind Spot
What others know about you, but you don't realize (e.g., a bad habit). We need feedback to fix this.
Hidden Area (Facade)
What you know about yourself, but keep private from others (e.g., fears, private goals).
Unknown Area
What nobody knows about you (hidden talents or subconscious feelings).
Activity:

We selected our top 5 strengths and asked our group members to select strengths for us. This helped us find our "Blind Spots" (hidden gems) and expand our "Open Area."

5. Role-Play Activity: Professional Skills in Action

In groups, we practiced how to use these skills in real workplace situations. We chose one of these scenarios:

Conflict Resolution
A team leader solving a fight between two team members.
Decision-Making
A team trying to agree on one option for a new product.
Customer Service
Handling an angry customer on the phone.

We learned that using the right communication and problem-solving skills is very important to handle these challenges.

Key Takeaways
  • Soft Skills Matter: Technical skills can get you an interview, but soft skills will get you the job and help you keep it.
  • Self-Awareness is Key: The Johari Window shows us that we need honest feedback from others to grow.
  • Inner to Outer: Our values and beliefs (roots and trunk) directly affect our character and actions (fruits) in the workplace.
Reflection & Application

How I Will Apply It:
I will try to expand my "Open Arena" by asking my friends and group members for honest feedback about my behavior and skills. I will also work on my "Growth Mindset" (a belief) so that when I fail at something, I see it as a chance to learn. Finally, I will remember to use good communication and listening skills when working in teams.

Module 02 · Lecture 02

Portfolio Management

Overview

This lesson was about how to collect and organize our work properly. The lecturer was Ms. Ishara Raviharee Weerasinghe. We learned what a portfolio is, why it is important, and the four main types of portfolios.

1. What is a Portfolio?

A portfolio is not just a list of skills like a CV. It is proof of what you can do.

A CV only tells people what you know. But a portfolio shows your real work. It helps people see your actual skills and how you solve problems. Good work can be easily lost if we don't keep it in a proper system. A portfolio helps us show our value to others.

2. Four Types of Portfolios

There are four main types of portfolios used across professional and academic careers:

Personal Portfolio
Shows creative work. Used by artists, designers, and writers to show their unique style and visual voice.
Career / Dossier Portfolio
Used for job applications and promotions. It has a CV, cover letter, references, and concrete work samples.
Employment Portfolio
Shows what you have done at your current job. It has certificates, milestone projects, and performance evaluations.
Assessment Portfolio
Shows how you are learning and growing over time. This current module portfolio is an Assessment Portfolio.
3. Where a Portfolio Helps
Job Applications
To show living proof of your technical skills to hiring managers.
Career Growth
To substantiate requests for promotions, salary raises, and leadership roles.
Education
For competitive graduate school, university admissions, and scholarship applications.
Freelancing
To attract new clients by demonstrating past successful deliverables.
Networking
To build your personal brand and credibility across industry events and online networks.
4. How to Build a Portfolio (6 Steps)

Building a portfolio is a deliberate step-by-step process:

01
Purpose
Ask yourself, "Why am I making this?"
02
Audience
Think about "Who will read it?"
03
Format
Decide if it is a printed book or a digital file / web portfolio.
04
Collect
Gather your work, certificates, project code, and artifacts.
05
Organise
Put them into clear sections with logical headings and flow.
06
Reflect
Explain why each item is important and what you learned from it.
5. Digital Footprint & Platforms
Your Digital Footprint:

You must be careful about what you post online. Keep a good, professional image on LinkedIn and GitHub. Do not post bad things on social media because employers can find them.

Online Tools:

You can use websites like LinkedIn, GitHub Pages (for coding), Behance (for art), Wix, or WordPress to build your portfolio.

AI Tools:

You can use AI (like ChatGPT or Grammarly) to check your spelling and make your writing better. But your work must be your own (original).

6. Class Activity: Newspaper Card Holder

In class, our group did a fun activity. We made a card holder using old newspapers.

  • Theme: "Education"
  • Design: We made a pocket for each group member to put their student ID card.
  • Key Lesson: This activity helped us understand how to organize different things in one place, just like a portfolio.
Key Takeaways
  • Proof, Not Just a List: A portfolio is proof of your skills, not just a list.
  • Context is Essential: Every item in the portfolio needs context (an explanation of why it is there).
  • Living Document: A portfolio is never finished. You must update it with new achievements.
Reflection & Application

How I Will Apply It:
I will keep all my certificates and project work in one safe place. When I apply for a job, I will build a special portfolio that matches that job. I will also be careful about my social media posts so that my online image stays professional.

Module 03 · Lecture 03

Interview & STAR

Overview

This lesson was about how to find a job and how to face a job interview successfully. We learned how to write a good CV, how to prepare for an interview, and what to do (and what not to do) during an interview to get the job we want.

1. The CV (Curriculum Vitae)

A CV is a formal document of 1 to 2 pages. It contains a detailed summary of a person's education, qualifications, experience, and accomplishments. It is like a written advertisement for yourself.

Key Tip:

Always start with your current or most recent job and work backward. Only include the most recent and relevant education. Tailor your CV to match the specific job you are applying for.

2. The Job Interview & Non-Verbal Impact

A job interview is a conversation between a job applicant and a company representative.

  • Why do we do it? The interviewer wants to know if you have the right skills and if you are the best match for the job. You want to know if the job, the team, and the working environment match your expectations.
  • Communication is Key: 93% of face-to-face communication is non-verbal!
55%
Body Language
Posture, eye contact, smiling, handshake
38%
Tone of Voice
Pacing, pitch, confidence, warmth
7%
Actual Words
The spoken verbal vocabulary
3. How to Prepare for an Interview

To do well, you must prepare before you go:

Research the Company
Know their history, mission, leadership, products, and recent industry news.
Understand the Job Description
Know exactly what technical and interpersonal skills they are looking for.
Prepare Your Answers
Practice common questions like "Tell me about yourself" and behavioral prompts.
Prepare Questions
Ask thoughtful questions about the team, tech stack, or culture to show genuine interest.
Dress Appropriately
Wear professional clothes. It is always better to be slightly overdressed than underdressed.
Arrive Early & Follow-Up
Plan to arrive 10-15 minutes early, and send a thank-you email within 24 hours.
4. Dressing and Grooming (Dos & Don'ts)
DO
  • Wear a well-fitting suit in neutral colors (black, navy, gray).
  • Keep your hair clean and neatly styled.
  • Use natural, light makeup.
  • Polish your formal footwear cleanly.
DON'T
  • Wear overly bright neon colors or flashy noisy jewelry.
  • Wear too much overpowering perfume or cologne.
  • Have messy untamed hair or wrinkled, untucked shirts.
  • Wear scuffed casual sneakers or sandals.
5. The STAR Method (Answering Questions)

When the interviewer asks about past experiences or challenges, organize your response with the STAR framework:

S
Situation
Give brief details about the specific situation or context.
T
Task
Say what you wanted to achieve (your objective or target).
A
Action
Say what you did (your specific individual steps).
R
Result
Say what the outcome was and quantify the positive impact.
Concrete Example:

"In my last project, we had a tight deadline (Situation). I needed to finish the report by 5 PM (Task). I organized my tasks and worked collaboratively with the team (Action). As a result, we delivered the complete work on time with zero errors (Result)."

6. 7 C's of Effective Communication
1. Clear – Present information clearly.
2. Concise – Don't talk too long; get straight to the point.
3. Correct – Avoid giving wrong or misleading facts.
4. Complete – Provide all necessary context and details.
5. Concrete – Give real examples, metrics, and stories.
6. Courteous – Always be respectful, polite, and attentive.
7. Confident – Radiate composure and a "can-do" mindset.
7. Mock Panel Self-Reflection
  • What were my strengths? (e.g., I maintained solid eye contact, smiled warmly, and kept composure).
  • What were my weaknesses? (e.g., I was slightly nervous initially and fidgeted with my hands).
  • How can I improve? (e.g., I will practice answering common behavioral questions with peers to build natural confidence).
  • Would I hire myself? (Yes, because I showed enthusiasm, authenticity, and used good examples to prove my technical and team abilities).
Key Takeaways
  • Preparation is Everything: Researching the company and practicing the STAR method builds unshakeable confidence.
  • Body Language Speaks Volumes: Your posture, eye contact, and tone are just as decisive as your technical words.
  • Honesty & Authenticity: Be genuine. When asked about weaknesses, mention an honest one and explain how you are proactively improving it.
Reflection & Application

How I Will Apply It:
I will create a tailored CV for every job I apply for, highlighting my most relevant skills. Before my next interview, I will research the company thoroughly and practice using the STAR method to answer behavioral questions. I will also make sure to dress professionally, arrive 15 minutes early, and send a thank-you email afterward to leave a good impression.

Module 04 · Lecture 04

CV Writing

Overview

This lesson was about how to create a good Curriculum Vitae (CV). A CV is your first chance to make a good impression on a company. We learned what to include in a CV, how to format it, and how to make it stand out from other applicants.

1. What is a CV?

A CV (Curriculum Vitae) is a formal document that summarizes your education, work experience, skills, and achievements.

Purpose
To show the employer that you are the right person for the specific job role.
Why it matters
Your CV is your first opportunity to impress a recruiter and get shortlisted for an interview. A well-crafted CV separates you from hundreds of applicants.
2. Tailoring Your CV

You should never use the exact same generic CV for every job. You must tailor (customize) your CV for each specific position:

  • Highlight Relevant Skills: If you apply for a marketing role, highlight your communication and creative campaigns. If you apply for a tech job, spotlight your coding stack, frameworks, and problem-solving metrics.
  • Do Your Research: Read the job description carefully. Integrate the exact keywords they emphasize. This ensures your CV passes automated computer filters (Applicant Tracking Systems - ATS) that companies use to screen applicants.
3. CV Structure and Formatting

A professional CV must look clean, balanced, and instantly legible:

Clear Hierarchy
Use distinct bold headings and concise bullet points rather than dense paragraphs.
White Space
Leave adequate margins and spacing so the page never looks cramped or overwhelming.
Consistent Polish
Use standard professional fonts and consistent sizing. Keep total length to 1–2 pages maximum.
4. Essential Parts of a CV
Personal Information
Full name, professional phone number, email address, location, LinkedIn URL, and portfolio link.
Professional Summary
A sharp 2-3 sentence overview highlighting your career focus, technical identity, and key value proposition.
Work Experience
Reverse chronological order. Job title, organization, dates, action verbs, and quantified achievements (e.g., "Increased sales by 20%").
Education
Reverse chronological order. Degree, institution, and expected or confirmed graduation year.
Skills
Segregated into Hard Technical Skills (e.g., Java, Python, React, SQL) and Soft Skills (e.g., Teamwork, Leadership, Communication).
Achievements & Awards
Academic honours, industry certifications, hackathon prizes, or recognized leadership roles.
5. Common CV Mistakes to Avoid
Pitfalls to Eliminate
  • Spelling & Grammar Errors: Even tiny typos make you appear careless. Always proofread multiple times.
  • Irrelevant Information: Exclude outdated, non-transferable odd jobs that add zero value to the target role.
  • Wall-of-Text Formatting: Never write long narrative blocks; recruiters scan bullets in under 10 seconds.
  • Inconsistent Styling: Mismatched fonts, irregular bullet indentation, or varying date formats look unprofessional.
Industry Best Practice
  • Maintain a comprehensive "Master CV" containing every accomplishment.
  • Derive a focused 1-2 page tailored version specifically mapped to each target job description.
  • Save and submit cleanly formatted PDF documents with ATS-friendly text layers.
Key Takeaways
  • First Impression: A CV is your primary marketing document; precision and visual polish are paramount.
  • Customization: Always align your keywords and highlighted projects with the specific vacancy.
  • Clear and Concise: Keep layout clean, easily scannable, and free of grammatical flaws.
Reflection & Application

How I Will Apply It:
I will create a master CV that has all my experience and skills. Then, every time I apply for a job, I will make a copy of it and change the "Professional Summary" and skills to match that specific job. I will also use a good online template to make it look professional and ask a friend to check it for spelling mistakes before I send it.

Module 05 · Lecture 05

Speaking Skills & Meetings

Overview

This lesson was about how to plan, run, and take part in professional meetings. We learned the vocabulary used in meetings, how to plan a meeting, how to be a good chairperson, and how to contribute effectively as a team member.

1. Essential Meeting Vocabulary
Chair a meeting
To be in charge of, lead, and facilitate the formal proceedings of the meeting.
Agenda
The structured list of topics and action items to be discussed in sequential order.
Minutes
The official written record of decisions made, discussions held, and assigned responsibilities.
To circulate
To distribute materials (such as the agenda or briefing docs) to all attendees beforehand.
To postpone / put back
To reschedule the meeting or a specific discussion item to a later date or time.
To digress
To wander off the agreed agenda topic and discuss unrelated or tangential matters.
To take the minutes
To act as the designated minute-taker recording motions, key arguments, and outcomes.
2. Types of Meetings
Brainstorming
Rapidly generating diverse creative solutions and exploring new concepts without immediate criticism.
Project / Team Meeting
Collaborative synchronization for members executing a specific technical sprint or milestone.
Departmental Meeting
Operational alignment within a dedicated functional unit (e.g., Engineering, Sales, QA).
Board Meeting
Formal executive assembly of company directors establishing high-level corporate governance.
AGM (Annual General Meeting)
Mandatory annual forum where company shareholders review audited accounts and company trajectory.
3. Planning a Meeting: 10-Point Checklist

Before scheduling any meeting, rigorous preparation is required:

1. Purpose: Why are we meeting?
2. Outcome: What tangible deliverable do we want to achieve?
3. Participants: Who strictly needs to be present?
4. Date & Location: When and where is the forum hosted?
5. Equipment: Do we need a projector, whiteboard, or dial-in link?
6. Time: What is the strict duration and agenda timing?
7. Style: Is it a strict formal parliamentary session or casual standup?
8. Papers: Are the agenda and past minutes circulated in advance?
9. Problems: What potential bottlenecks or conflicts could arise?
4. The Role of the Chairperson (Facilitator)

The person leading the meeting acts like a diplomatic referee ensuring fairness and order:

  • Time Management: Strictly keeping an eye on the clock and pacing each agenda item.
  • Inclusive Participation: Ensuring everyone gets an opportunity to contribute, especially quieter participants.
  • Managing Digressions: Tactfully halting off-topic arguments and pulling the team back to core objectives.
  • Driving Decisions: Clarifying consensus and summarizing concrete action items before adjourning.
5. How to Contribute Effectively (DOs & DON'Ts)
DO's
  • Listen actively and maintain a constructive, positive posture.
  • Stay disciplined and stick to the current agenda item.
  • Express ideas using clear, simple, and diplomatic language.
  • Allow colleagues to complete their thoughts before speaking.
  • Ask clarifying questions politely when requirements are ambiguous.
DON'Ts
  • Don't monopolize or hijack the floor with endless talking.
  • Don't become emotional, defensive, or overly confrontational.
  • Don't direct personal criticisms or insults at teammates.
  • Don't repeatedly interrupt speakers while they present arguments.
6. Group Activity: Running a Meeting

In class, we organized practical simulation teams to:

  • Identify different meeting categories and match them with appropriate institutional goals.
  • Author a professional sample Agenda for a sprint progress review.
  • Simulate resolving common meeting hurdles (e.g., talkative hijackers, silent attendees, digression).
  • Design a concise evaluation feedback form to audit meeting effectiveness.
Key Takeaways
  • Preparation is Key: A productive meeting starts with an advance agenda and clear deliverables.
  • Time is Valuable: The chairperson must guard everyone's time by starting and adjourning on schedule.
  • Respect Others: Active listening, disciplined contributions, and zero interruptions define meeting maturity.
Reflection & Application

How I Will Apply It:
In the future, when I am in a team meeting, I will make sure to listen actively and not interrupt others. If I have to lead a meeting, I will prepare a clear agenda beforehand and send it to everyone. I will also act as a good "referee" to make sure everyone gets a chance to speak and that we stick to the time limit.

Module 06 · Lecture 06

Email & Online Etiquette

Overview

This lesson was about how to write professional emails and how to behave properly in an online or office environment. We learned the rules of email etiquette, how to structure an email, and how to use the right tone when talking to teachers, bosses, or clients.

1. What is Etiquette?

Etiquette is the set of established rules that govern the "correct" and respectful way to behave in a professional community.

Email Etiquette is crucial in the modern digital workplace. How you construct messages to your supervisors, lecturers, or corporate clients is a direct reflection of your personal character and credibility.

2. Basic Rules of Email Etiquette
1. Subject Line
Always include a concise, informative subject (e.g., "Interview Request" or "Absence Notification"). Never leave it blank.
2. Proper Greeting
Use titles and last names (e.g., "Dear Dr. Silva,"). Never use casual greetings like "Hey" with professors or superiors.
3. NO ALL CAPS
Typing in all capital letters reads like shouting/screaming at the recipient. Maintain normal casing.
4. Privacy & Data
Never transmit confidential bank details, passwords, or personal credentials over insecure email threads.
5. No Slang or Emojis
Avoid chat abbreviations like "LOL", "IDK", or smiley faces in formal correspondence. Use full words.
6. Proofread Grammar
Always spell-check before sending. Small grammatical mistakes create an impression of carelessness.
7. Zero Junk Forwarding
Do not forward chain letters, viral jokes, or spam to academic or corporate email lists.
8. 24-Hour THINK Rule
If feeling upset or angry, wait 24 hours before sending. Never use email as a weapon to argue or vent.
9. Professional Address
Use a clear professional address (e.g., name@domain.com). Avoid adolescent handles like "coolguy99@...".
3. Structure of a Professional Email (5 Parts)
To: Full verified email address of the designated recipient.
Subject Line: High-clarity summary of message intent.
Greeting: Formal respectful salutation ("Dear Ms. Weerasinghe,").
Body: The core message, formatted with polite paragraph breaks.
Closing: Professional sign-off ("Sincerely," or "Best regards,") followed by Full Name and ID.
4. Polite Tone: How to Ask for Things
Don't Say
  • "I want to make an appointment with you tomorrow at 12:00."
  • "I don't understand the lecture. Explain it more."
  • "Send me the assignment notes now."
Do Say
  • "Would it be possible to schedule a brief appointment with you this week? I am free Tuesday afternoon or Thursday morning."
  • "I am still a bit confused by the lecture concepts. Could we meet to discuss them further?"
  • "Could you kindly share the assignment guidelines at your earliest convenience?"
5. Social Media Etiquette & Digital Footprint
  • Recruiters and universities actively inspect public social media profiles (LinkedIn, Facebook, Instagram) during background vetting.
  • Even after securing employment, inappropriate public posts can lead to immediate contract termination.
  • Proactively utilize social platforms to showcase technical projects and build an inspiring personal brand.
Key Takeaways
  • Written Presence Matters: Every email you send establishes your professional reputation.
  • THINK Before Sending: True emotional composure means never hitting send while angry.
  • Tone of Diplomacy: Polite phrasing turns potential friction into productive teamwork.
Reflection & Application

How I Will Apply It:
When writing emails to professors or prospective employers, I will use clear subject lines, formal greetings, and polite phrasing. I will avoid casual slang and proofread carefully. I will also maintain a respectful social media presence that reflects my professional values.

Module 07 · Lecture 07

Telephone Etiquette & Customer Service

Overview

This lesson was about how to talk on the phone in a professional way. When we talk to customers on the phone, we represent our company. A good phone call can make a customer happy, but a bad phone call can make them leave. We learned the 7 steps to perfect telephone etiquette and how to handle difficult callers.

1. Why is Telephone Etiquette Important?
6 Sec
First Impressions
It takes only 6 seconds for a caller to formulate an impression about your business and competence.
100%
Company Image
Every time you answer a business line, you represent the total reputation of the entire organization.
75%
Customer Satisfaction
75% of adults use their phones everywhere; customers expect immediate, polite, professional assistance.
2. The 7 Steps to Perfect Telephone Etiquette
1. Answer Promptly
Pick up within 3 rings with an upbeat, warm tone. (Tip: Smile before picking up — it physically warms your voice!).
2. Introduce Yourself
Greeting, department, and your name (e.g., "Good morning, IT Department, this is Sarah. How may I help you?").
3. Give Full Attention
Zero multi-tasking. Focus exclusively on the caller, not on side conversations or office distractions.
4. Take Notes & Repeat
Keep pen and paper ready. Record crucial details and repeat them back to guarantee mutual understanding.
5. Avoid Bad Habits
Never eat, chew gum, or drink while speaking. Callers hear every sound and find it disrespectful.
6. Resolve Immediately
Solve issues on the call when possible. Keep a quick directory sheet of key departments and contacts handy.
7. End on Positive Note
Summarize agreed next action steps and thank the caller warmly for reaching out.
3. Putting a Caller on Hold (5 Mandatory Rules)

If you need to investigate information, observe these 5 hold protocols:

1. Ask for Permission: "May I put you on hold?"
2. Wait for Response: Listen for agreement before pressing hold.
3. Explain Why: Tell them exactly what you are verifying.
4. Give Time Frame: State duration (e.g., "About 1 minute").
5. Thank Them: Upon return, always say "Thank you for holding."

Golden Rule: Never leave a caller on hold for more than 30–60 seconds without checking back.

4. Transferring a Call Protocol
  • Clearly explain to the caller why the transfer is necessary.
  • Confirm the receiving party is present and ready to take the call before transferring.
  • Always give the caller the specific colleague's name and direct extension in case the call disconnects.
5. Handling Angry Callers (Customer Care Diplomacy)
Listen Fully
Let the caller vent without interrupting or defending prematurely.
Show Empathy
Say: "I am truly sorry to hear that; please tell me what happened."
Stay Objective
Do not take frustration personally; they are mad at the situation, not you.
Document Details
Write down exact details to ensure resolution steps are accurate.
Be Solutions-Oriented
Never say "That's not my job." Say: "Let me find the right person to solve this."
Stay Calm
Never match the customer's anger or argue over the telephone.
6. Proper Telephone Language (Do's & Don'ts)
Don't Say
  • "Hang on."
  • "Who's calling?"
  • "I can't hear you, speak up!"
  • "Yeah" or "Huh?"
Do Say
  • "May I put you on hold for a moment?"
  • "May I say who is calling, please?"
  • "I am having a little difficulty hearing you. Could you please speak up slightly?"
  • "Yes, certainly." or "I understand."
Key Takeaways
  • Professionalism: Your tone, pacing, and vocabulary are your brand ambassadors.

Module 08 · Lecture 08

Emotional Intelligence

Overview

This lesson was about Emotional Intelligence (EQ). We learned what emotions are, how our brain creates them, and why EQ is sometimes more important than IQ (Intelligence Quotient) for success in the workplace. We also looked at how to manage our own emotions and understand the emotions of others.

1. What are Emotions?

An emotion is a complex psychological state comprising three interconnected elements:

Subjective Experience
How you interpret and feel the internal experience within yourself.
Physiological Response
How your autonomic biology responds (e.g., accelerated heart rate, adrenaline).
Behavioural Response
How you externally express it through facial expressions, tears, or posture.

Why do we have emotions? Emotions are biological adaptations that ensure evolutionary survival (e.g., fear triggers retreat from physical danger) and enable non-verbal human social bonding.

2. The 6 Basic (Hard-wired) Emotions

According to psychologist Paul Ekman, human beings are universally born with 6 innate, hard-wired emotions:

Joy (Happiness)
Sadness
Anger
Fear
Disgust
Surprise

Note: Complex interpersonal traits like empathy, patience, and gratitude are "taught emotions" cultivated through socialization and practice.

3. The Brain and Emotions (The 3 Brains)
Reptilian Complex
Instinctive survival functions, breathing, heart rate, fight-or-flight reflex.
Limbic System
The emotional mammalian center housing the Amygdala (fear, threat perception, emotional memories).
Neocortex
The Prefrontal Cortex (the "CEO" of the brain) governing logic, deliberation, reasoning, and ethics.
The Amygdala Hijack:

When acute anger or fear triggers the Amygdala, it bypasses the logical Prefrontal Cortex. This physiological short-circuit is called an "Amygdala Hijack", causing impulsive emotional outbursts ("shoot first and ask questions later"). Because the teenage prefrontal cortex is still maturing, intentional pausing techniques are essential to let logic catch up.

4. What is EQ (Emotional Intelligence)?

EQ is the ability to recognize, understand, and regulate emotions in yourself and in others to steer positive outcomes.

IQ (Intelligence Quotient)
Measures logic, math, and technical cognitive capabilities. Largely fixed from birth.
EQ (Emotional Intelligence)
Measures emotional self-governance and interpersonal agility. Can be actively trained and expanded throughout lifetime.

Workplace Impact: Studies demonstrate that 58% of professional career success is determined by EQ. High EQ professionals remain calm under fire, resolve team disputes swiftly, and excel in engineering leadership.

5. The 4 Components of Emotional Intelligence
1. Self-Awareness
Recognizing your emotions in real time; identifying personal triggers, strengths, and blind spots.
2. Self-Management
Mastering impulse control. Remaining composed, adaptable, and composed during stressful crises.
3. Social Awareness (Empathy)
Reading room dynamics and accurately sensing colleagues' unspoken feelings by stepping into their shoes.
4. Relationship Management
Communicating clearly, inspiring peers, mitigating team friction, and building enduring professional alliances.
6. Emotional Contagion

Human emotions are biologically contagious. A cheerful, composed leader projects calm and enthusiasm across the entire team, whereas panic or cynicism poisons team morale. Mindful professionals harness positive contagion to boost team output.

Key Takeaways
  • EQ is Crucial: Technical expertise opens doors; emotional intelligence drives long-term career promotions.
  • Control the Amygdala: Pausing to take a deliberate breath allows the prefrontal cortex to prevent regrettable outbursts.
  • Empathy Matters: Authentic empathy is the cornerstone of respected engineering leadership.
Reflection & Application

How I Will Apply It:
In the future, when I am working on a stressful project or facing a conflict in a team, I will try to control my "Amygdala Hijack" by taking a moment to pause before reacting. I will practice empathy by listening to my teammates' problems and trying to understand their point of view. I will also try to spread positive energy (emotional contagion) so my team feels motivated and happy.

Dining Etiquette & Table Manners

Overview

This lesson was about how to behave properly at the dining table. Good table manners show respect, education, and professionalism. Whether you are eating with friends, family, or business clients, knowing how to use your utensils and behave at the table is very important.

1. Why is Dining Etiquette Important?
Social Ritual
Dining is never merely about food; it is a shared ritual of mutual respect and social cohesion.
Executive Presence
Your table manners reveal your upbringing, cultural education, and situational consideration.
Comfort & Trust
Recruiters observe candidates at meals to assess whether they can represent the firm before high-profile clients.
2. Table Settings & Utensils
  • The Golden Rule: Always begin with utensils positioned on the outside and work your way inward with each successive course. The outermost fork is designated for the appetizer or salad.
  • The "b" and "d" Trick: Form a circle touching thumb to index finger on both hands. Your left hand spells a "b" for bread (bread plate is on your left). Your right hand spells a "d" for drink (beverage glass is on your right).
  • Passing Protocol: Always pass salt and pepper shakers together as a pair. Pass all dishes around the table to the right (counter-clockwise).
3. Essential Rules of the Table
The Napkin
Place on your lap immediately upon seating (never tuck as a bib). When temporarily leaving, place it on your chair; upon departure, place loosely on the left of the plate.
Bread Protocol
Tear bread into bite-sized pieces with your fingers; never cut with a knife. Butter only the individual piece you are about to consume.
Soup Etiquette
Spoon soup away from yourself (like a ship sailing out). Never slurp; gently tip the bowl away from you for the final spoonful.
U.F.O. (Unidentified Food Objects)
If encountering an inedible bone or gristle, discreetly remove it using the same fork/spoon that placed it in your mouth. Never spit into napkins or pick teeth at the table.
Sneezing & Coughing
Turn away from food, shielding with a napkin. If you must blow your nose, excuse yourself quietly to the restroom.
4. Dining Styles: American vs. European
American ("Zig-Zag") Style

Cut food with knife in right hand and fork in left. Rest the knife on top edge of plate, switch fork to dominant right hand, and eat with tines pointing upward.

European (Continental) Style

Keep fork permanently in left hand (tines facing down) and knife in right hand throughout the meal. Food is conveyed to mouth without ever switching hands.

5. Resting Positions & Meal Completion
Resting (Still Eating)
American: Knife rested at top edge of plate (blade inward), fork placed beside it tines up.
European: Cross knife and fork in an inverted "V" with fork tines facing downward.
Finished (4:25 Clock Position)
Lay knife and fork parallel across the center of the plate angled toward the 4:20 or 4:25 position. Fork tines face upward; knife blade faces inward.
6. Thank You Notes

Following any hosted business dinner or formal banquet, it is customary to send a prompt, handwritten "Thank You" note expressing genuine gratitude and citing a memorable conversation topic.

Key Takeaways
  • Respect & Comfort: True dining etiquette puts fellow table companions immediately at ease.
  • Follow the Host: Always wait for the host or hostess to begin eating before lifting utensils.
  • Attention to Nuance: Mastering small customs like tearing bread and placing napkins signals executive polish.
Reflection & Application

How I Will Apply It:
In the future, if I attend a business dinner or a formal event, I will remember to use the "outside-in" rule for my utensils and put my napkin on my lap immediately. I will also make sure to break my bread with my hands instead of cutting it. If I am unsure about anything, I will quietly watch the host or other people at the table and follow their lead to avoid making mistakes.
